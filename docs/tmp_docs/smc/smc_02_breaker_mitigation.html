Breaker and Mitigation Blocks

When a BOS breaks through an order block — which in Flint is simply our supply or demand zone — that zone flips polarity. Understanding how a zone was broken determines whether it becomes a breaker block or a mitigation block — and how strongly it will react on retest. Note: throughout this section, reference images use SMC vocabulary ("order block" / "OB"). In every case, this means our supply/demand zone.

Key terms used throughout this section

Displacement — a strong, impulsive price move characterised by large-body candles with little or no overlap between consecutive candle bodies. Displacement shows aggressive institutional order flow. In Flint's HA system, displacement appears as a run of same-colour HA candles with expanding bodies. A move without displacement (small bodies, heavy overlap, grinding) is not a valid structural break — it lacks institutional conviction.

Fair Value Gap (FVG) — a three-candle pattern where the middle candle's body is so large that a gap exists between the wicks of candle 1 and candle 3 (they don't overlap). This gap represents unfilled institutional orders — price tends to return to fill the gap before continuing. FVGs inside breaker or mitigation block zones significantly strengthen the signal because two sources of institutional order flow are stacked at the same level. In Flint, FVG detection is a candle-triplet check: candle_1_high < candle_3_low (bullish FVG) or candle_1_low > candle_3_high (bearish FVG).

Zone freshness — a zone that has never been retested since formation is "fresh" and carries the highest probability of reaction. Each retest consumes some of the unfilled orders at that level. In Flint, track retest count per zone: first-touch zones get full weight, second-touch zones are reduced, and zones with 3+ retests should be considered exhausted and removed from the active stack.

Order block (OB) = our supply/demand zone. In SMC literature, "order block" is the standard term for what Flint already tracks as supply and demand zones. They are the same thing — the consolidation range where institutional orders were placed before a displacement move. In Flint's HA system, each HA color flip creates a zone; that zone is an order block. Throughout this document, the reference images and SMC descriptions use "order block" / "OB" because that is standard SMC vocabulary. When reading, mentally substitute "our supply/demand zone" wherever you see "OB" or "order block." The lifecycle: an active (unbroken) OB = a live supply or demand zone in Flint's tracking stack. When price closes through it (body close beyond the zone boundary), the OB is consumed/invalidated and is either reclassified as a breaker block or mitigation block (see below), or simply removed from the active stack.

Zone polarity flip mechanics

The core mechanic
Bullish demand → broken by bearish BOS → becomes bearish breaker/mitigation block (resistance on retest)
Bearish supply → broken by bullish BOS → becomes bullish breaker/mitigation block (support on retest)

In Flint terms: H1 demand zones broken by a bearish BOS become the zones you short into on retest. Same price level, opposite polarity. The zone flipped because the order flow behind it flipped.
Zone lifecycle — from active OB to breaker or mitigation
Active order block → price closes through it (body close beyond the zone boundary = consumed / invalidated, marked ✗ on the panoramic chart) → if the close is accompanied by a full structural reversal (BOS/MSS with displacement), the consumed zone flips polarity and becomes a breaker block on retest. If the zone is consumed without a full reversal (failure swing only), it becomes a mitigation block. In Flint's zone tracking: consumed zones are removed from the active supply/demand stack and reclassified as breaker or mitigation depending on the structural context.

Breaker block — definition and mechanics

A breaker block forms when a zone is broken through a full structural reversal. Price makes a HH then breaks all the way below the previous LL (or makes a LL then breaks above the previous HH). The intermediate HL zone (between HH and LL) becomes the breaker block.

⚙️ Mechanical Definition — Breaker Block (for Flint implementation)

Core principle: A breaker block is a failed order block — a zone that was initially created as supply or demand, then invalidated by a full structural reversal (BOS / Market Structure Shift). After invalidation, the zone flips polarity and acts as the opposite type of S/R on retest.

Formation sequence — Bullish Breaker:

  1. Market is in a bearish leg — making LL after LL.
  2. A new LL forms, taking sell-side liquidity below previous lows.
  3. Price fails to continue bearish — instead breaks above the previous LH, creating a bullish Market Structure Shift (MSS / BOS).
  4. The last down-close (bearish) candle before the displacement move = the Bullish Breaker Block.
  5. On retest from above, this zone acts as support. Entry long at zone bottom, SL below zone low.

Formation sequence — Bearish Breaker:

  1. Market is in a bullish leg — making HH after HH.
  2. A new HH forms, taking buy-side liquidity above previous highs.
  3. Price fails to continue bullish — instead breaks below the previous HL, creating a bearish MSS / BOS.
  4. The last up-close (bullish) candle before the displacement move = the Bearish Breaker Block.
  5. On retest from below, this zone acts as resistance. Entry short at zone top, SL above zone high.

5-point quality filter (high-probability breakers only):

  1. Liquidity grab before formation — a valid breaker must take liquidity (sell-side for bullish / buy-side for bearish) before flipping.
  2. Clear MSS / BOS — the breaker is confirmed only when price closes past the opposing swing (LH for bullish, HL for bearish), showing displacement.
  3. Displacement candle after break — a strong impulsive move away from the breaker (large displacement candle) shows smart money involvement.
  4. Mitigation retest — price should retrace back into the breaker block zone with precision (wick or body touch) before continuation.
  5. HTF alignment — high-probability breakers line up with the higher-timeframe order flow (e.g. the breaker sits inside or adjacent to an active HTF zone of matching polarity).

Breaker block during volatility — BMS mechanics

During high-volatility events (news, session opens), breaker blocks form with a characteristic "sharp move and BMS" (Break of Market Structure) pattern. The speed of the BMS is what creates the zone — price moves so fast that it leaves an imbalance, and the zone it broke through becomes the breaker block.

Mitigation block — definition and entry

A mitigation block forms from a failure swing — price makes a lower high (fails to make new HH) but does NOT break to a new LL. No full reversal. The high before the failure is the mitigation block. Trapped traders exit when price returns.

⚙️ Mechanical Definition — Mitigation Block (for Flint implementation)

Core principle: A mitigation block is a failure swing zone — price attempts to continue the current trend but fails to break a key high or low. This failure shows weakened momentum and a possible shift in market structure. Unlike a breaker block, no liquidity grab precedes the formation and no full structural reversal occurs. The zone represents unfilled institutional orders from the failed move.

Graduation to breaker: A mitigation block is sometimes called an "immature breaker block". If structure subsequently breaks fully through the mitigation zone with displacement (completing the reversal that the original failure swing started), the zone graduates to a breaker block and should be reclassified with higher confidence weight. In Flint, this means upgrading the zone type from mitigation to breaker in the active tracking stack.

Formation sequence — Bullish Mitigation Block:

  1. Price is in a downtrend — making LL after LL.
  2. Price dips into a demand / order block zone but does NOT break the previous low.
  3. Momentum shifts upward, breaking the last lower high.
  4. The candle or zone before the shift (at the failure swing low) = the Bullish Mitigation Block.
  5. When price revisits this level, it acts as support — unfilled buy orders absorb selling pressure.

Formation sequence — Bearish Mitigation Block:

  1. Price is in an uptrend — making HH after HH.
  2. Price reaches a supply zone but cannot continue higher — fails to make new HH.
  3. Price then breaks below the previous higher low.
  4. The candle or zone at the failure swing high (the LH) = the Bearish Mitigation Block.
  5. When price revisits this level, it acts as resistance — trapped longs and institutional sells absorb buying pressure.

Confirmation strengthener: A Fair Value Gap (FVG) that overlaps with the mitigation block significantly strengthens the signal. The FVG represents institutional orders waiting at that level — when both align, the probability of reaction increases substantially. Note: FVG overlap also strengthens breaker block signals (see the breaker 5-point quality filter above), but the effect is more critical for mitigation blocks because they are inherently weaker zones — without FVG confirmation, mitigation block entries carry higher failure risk.

Breaker vs mitigation — the key difference

⚙️ Breaker Block vs Mitigation Block — Mechanical Comparison (for Flint implementation)
Feature Breaker Block Mitigation Block
Formation trigger Order block INVALIDATED by full structural reversal (BOS/MSS) Failure swing — price fails to break a key high or low (failed continuation)
Liquidity interaction YES — liquidity is taken (sell-side for bullish, buy-side for bearish) BEFORE the flip NO — failure occurs WITHOUT a liquidity grab. No breakout then reversal.
Structural reversal FULL reversal — BOS confirmed (price breaks past opposing swing level) PARTIAL — momentum shifts but the higher-level structure has NOT fully reversed
Zone identification Last opposite candle before the displacement move (bullish breaker = last bearish candle, bearish breaker = last bullish candle) The candle/zone at the failure swing point (the LH for bearish, the HL for bullish)
Reaction strength STRONG — more traders trapped, institutional order flow fully reversed. Produces fast, decisive reactions. MODERATE — can produce false bounces because higher-level structure has not fully reversed. Requires FVG confirmation.
Flint use case Primary entry signal. Higher confidence, wider SL acceptable, larger TP target. Secondary / confirmation signal. Lower confidence, tighter SL, conservative TP, faster break-even.
Zone lifetime Typically one retest — breaker zones are consumed on first mitigation May see multiple weak retests before zone is consumed or broken through

Key implementation note: In the Flint rule engine, breaker blocks and mitigation blocks should be tracked as separate zone types with different confidence weights. When scoring entry signals, a breaker block retest should carry approximately 2× the weight of a mitigation block retest. Both zone types become invalid (removed from active tracking) once price closes cleanly through them.

Zone count timing: In the Spring Leaf H1 zone count framework (1–5 impulse, 6 boundary, 7–8 correction), breaker blocks are most likely to form at structural inflection points — especially near zone 6 (boundary) and during terminal exhaustion (zones 7–8), where full trend reversals occur. Mitigation blocks are more common mid-impulse (zones 2–5), where momentum pauses or weakens without fully reversing. Weighting entry signals by zone count position increases filtering accuracy.

Breaker Block vs Mitigation Block — structural comparison

Left (Breaker Block): High → Higher High → Higher Low (zone) → Lower Low (full reversal BOS). The HL zone is the breaker block. A full structural reversal occurred — price made HH then broke below prior LL. Right (Mitigation Block): High → Higher High → Lower High ("No Break" — failed HH). The LH is the mitigation block — a failure swing. LL was broken to create a downswing but the HH level was never exceeded.

Breaker block condition: Full trend reversal required. Price must make HH THEN break below prior LL (bearish). If the prior LL is not broken, it's a mitigation, not a breaker.
Mitigation block condition: Failure swing only. Price makes LH without breaking HH ("No Break"). The LH zone is the mitigation block. LL IS broken, but HH is not — partial structural shift only.
Trading implication: Breaker blocks produce stronger, faster reactions — more traders trapped, institutional order flow fully reversed. Mitigation blocks can produce false bounces because higher-level structure has not fully reversed.

Breaker block quality — weak BOS vs strong break

Best breaker blocks — quality factors from weak to strong

Not all breaker blocks are equal. A "BOS (Weak)" = small break, lower follow-through probability. A "BREAK (STRONG)" = high-momentum structural break. The best breaker blocks combine: (1) High BOS / liquidity sweep, (2) Strong break (large body, high momentum), (3) Imbalance/FVG in the zone, (4) Strong S/R flip (zone was previously significant structure).

Weak BOS: Small, tentative break. Breaker block forms with less institutional conviction. Reactions are smaller and less reliable. Use these only with additional confluence.
Strong break: Large momentum-driven BOS with significant candle body. Breaker block backed by strong institutional order flow. Sharp, fast reactions on retest.
IMB (imbalance/FVG): Unfilled institutional order flow inside the zone. Price is drawn back to fill it, which also triggers the breaker block reaction simultaneously.
S/R Flip: Zone was previously significant support/resistance with multiple touches. Multi-touch history dramatically increases reaction probability on the polarity-flipped retest.
